INTELLIGENT DOCUMENT Q&A SYSTEM USING RETRIEVAL
AUGMENTED GENERATION (RAG)
ABSTRACT
Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) has emerged as a promising approach to
address one of the most fundamental limitations of large language models: the inability
to reliably access and reason over external, domain-specific, or recently published
knowledge. By combining dense vector retrieval with generative language models,
RAG systems ground their outputs in verifiable source documents, significantly
reducing hallucination and improving factual accuracy.
In this project, we propose the design and implementation of a web-based Intelligent
Document Q&A system that leverages RAG architecture to enable natural language
querying over user-uploaded PDF documents. The system targets students and
researchers who need to extract specific information from academic papers and reports
without reading entire documents. Users upload their documents through a web
interface, which are then processed, chunked, and indexed into a vector database. At
query time, semantically relevant passages are retrieved and supplied as context to
Anthropic's Claude API, which generates accurate, grounded responses alongside the
source passages used.
The main contributions of this project are: a fully functional RAG pipeline covering
document ingestion, semantic chunking, embedding-based retrieval, and contextaugmented generation; a clean web interface supporting real-time document upload and
conversational querying; a source-attribution mechanism that displays the retrieved
passages behind each answer to ensure transparency; and a comparative evaluation of
the system against a no-retrieval baseline, examining the effect of chunk size on answer
quality. The project draws on recent advances in the field, including Self-RAG,
Adaptive-RAG, GraphRAG, and contextual retrieval techniques, and is implemented
using Python, FastAPI, FAISS, and the Anthropic Claude API.

INTRODUCTION
1. Introduction
The exponential growth of digital documents — research papers, technical reports,
manuals, and academic literature — has created a significant challenge: how can users
efficiently extract relevant information from large collections of unstructured text?
Traditional keyword-based search engines return documents but do not synthesize
answers, placing the burden of comprehension on the user. Meanwhile, recent advances
in Large Language Models (LLMs) have demonstrated remarkable natural language
understanding and generation capabilities, yet these models suffer from a welldocumented limitation known as hallucination — the tendency to generate plausiblesounding but factually incorrect information, particularly when queried about specific or
recent knowledge not present in their training data.
Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) has emerged as a powerful paradigm that
addresses this limitation by grounding LLM responses in retrieved, verifiable context.
Rather than relying solely on parametric knowledge encoded in model weights, RAG
systems dynamically retrieve relevant document passages at inference time and supply
them as context to the language model. This hybrid approach combines the fluency and
reasoning ability of generative models with the precision and verifiability of
information retrieval systems.
This report presents the first phase of a project aimed at building an intelligent, webbased Document Q&A system leveraging RAG architecture and Anthropic's Claude
API. The system will allow users to upload academic documents or research reports in
PDF format and interact with them through a natural language interface, receiving
accurate, context-grounded answers with source references.

BACKGROUND
2. Background
This section provides the necessary technical context for understanding the motivation
and design of the proposed system. It begins with an overview of large language models
and their known limitations, followed by a discussion of information retrieval
techniques and how they evolved toward dense vector search, and concludes with an
introduction to the RAG paradigm that bridges these two areas.
2.1 Large Language Models and Their Limitations
Large Language Models such as GPT-4 (OpenAI, 2023), Claude (Anthropic, 2023), and
LLaMA (Meta AI, 2023) are transformer-based neural networks trained on massive
corpora of text data. They have demonstrated strong performance across a wide range of
natural language processing tasks including summarization, translation, question
answering, and code generation. However, their knowledge is static — frozen at the
time of training — and they lack access to private, domain-specific, or recently
published information. This creates two core problems in knowledge-intensive
applications: knowledge staleness and hallucination.
Hallucination, in particular, is a critical concern for high-stakes domains such as
academic research, healthcare, and legal analysis. Studies have shown that LLMs can
confidently fabricate citations, misattribute findings, and generate incorrect factual
claims (Ji et al., 2023). Fine-tuning on domain-specific data is one mitigation strategy,
but it is computationally expensive and does not scale well to rapidly changing
knowledge bases. 
7
2.2 Information Retrieval and Vector Search
Classical information retrieval (IR) systems rely on lexical matching techniques such as
TF-IDF and BM25, which match query terms to document terms. While effective for
keyword search, these methods fail to capture semantic similarity — a query about
"cardiac arrest" may not retrieve a document discussing "heart failure" despite their
conceptual overlap.
Dense vector retrieval, enabled by neural embedding models, addresses this limitation
by mapping both queries and documents into a shared high-dimensional semantic space.
Similarity between a query and a document is then computed as the cosine similarity (or
dot product) between their vector representations. This approach, popularized by the
Dense Passage Retrieval (DPR) model (Karpukhin et al., 2020), significantly
outperforms lexical methods on open-domain question answering benchmarks.
2.3 The RAG Paradigm
RAG was formally introduced by Lewis et al. (2020) in their seminal paper "RetrievalAugmented Generation for Knowledge-Intensive NLP Tasks." The framework combines
a parametric memory component (the LLM) with a non-parametric memory component
(a retrieval system over a document index). At inference time, the model retrieves the
top-k most relevant passages for a given query and conditions its generation on both the
query and the retrieved passages. This simple yet powerful idea has since spawned a
rich line of research and industrial adoption.

LITERATURE REVIEW
3. Literature Review
The body of research on RAG has grown substantially since its introduction in 2020,
spanning foundational retrieval methods, architectural innovations, evaluation
frameworks, and domain-specific applications. This section surveys the most relevant
works, organized chronologically and thematically, to situate the proposed project
within the current state of the field.
3.1 Foundational Work
Lewis et al. (2020) introduced the original RAG framework, training end-to-end models
that jointly learned to retrieve and generate. They demonstrated significant
improvements over purely parametric models on open-domain QA tasks such as Natural
Questions and TriviaQA. This work established the conceptual foundation that
separating knowledge storage (retrieval index) from reasoning (LLM) leads to more
factually accurate and updatable systems.
Karpukhin et al. (2020) proposed Dense Passage Retrieval (DPR), showing that dense
embeddings trained with a simple bi-encoder architecture substantially outperform
BM25 for passage retrieval. DPR became the de facto retrieval backbone for many early
RAG systems and remains influential in modern implementations. 
9
3.2 Advances in RAG Architecture (2021–2023)
Borgeaud et al. (2022) introduced RETRO (Retrieval-Enhanced Transformer), which
incorporated retrieval not just at the input level but throughout the model's layers via
chunked cross-attention. RETRO achieved GPT-3-level performance with significantly
fewer parameters by offloading knowledge storage to a retrieval database,
demonstrating the efficiency gains possible through retrieval augmentation.
Izacard et al. (2021) proposed Fusion-in-Decoder (FiD), a method where multiple
retrieved passages are encoded independently but fused during the decoder stage. This
approach proved especially effective for multi-hop reasoning tasks where the answer
requires synthesizing information from several documents.
Shi et al. (2023) investigated the problem of irrelevant context distraction, finding that
LLMs can be negatively affected by retrieved passages that are superficially related but
do not contain the answer. Their work highlighted the importance of retrieval quality
and re-ranking as critical components of RAG pipelines.
3.3 Advanced RAG Variants (2023–2024)
The rapid proliferation of RAG research has led to a taxonomy of increasingly
sophisticated architectures:
Gao et al. (2023) provided a comprehensive survey of RAG techniques, categorizing
them into Naive RAG, Advanced RAG, and Modular RAG. Naive RAG refers to the
straightforward retrieve-then-generate pipeline. Advanced RAG introduces pre-retrieval
steps (query rewriting, HyDE — hypothetical document embeddings) and post-retrieval
steps (re-ranking, context compression). Modular RAG treats each component as a
replaceable module, enabling flexible composition. 
10
Asai et al. (2023) proposed Self-RAG, a framework where the LLM itself learns to
decide when to retrieve, what to retrieve, and how to critique its own generated output
using special reflection tokens. Self-RAG demonstrated that adaptive retrieval — only
retrieving when necessary — can outperform always-retrieving baselines.
Jeong et al. (2024) introduced Adaptive-RAG, which dynamically selects the retrieval
strategy (no retrieval, single-step, or multi-step) based on the complexity of the
incoming query, using a lightweight classifier trained on query difficulty. This work
addressed the efficiency-accuracy trade-off in RAG systems.
Edge et al. (2024) from Microsoft Research proposed GraphRAG, which constructs a
knowledge graph from the document corpus and uses graph-based community detection
to create hierarchical summaries, enabling more effective retrieval for queries that
require a global understanding of the document collection rather than pinpointed
passage lookup.
3.4 Evaluation and Benchmarking
Es et al. (2023) introduced RAGAS (Retrieval Augmented Generation Assessment), a
framework for evaluating RAG pipelines along three dimensions: faithfulness (is the
answer grounded in the retrieved context?), answer relevance (does the answer address
the question?), and context relevance (was the retrieved context actually useful?).
RAGAS has become a standard evaluation toolkit in the field.
Chen et al. (2024) conducted a systematic benchmarking study comparing different
chunking strategies, embedding models, and retrieval methods across multiple
document types. They found that chunk size has a significant impact on performance:
smaller chunks (128–256 tokens) favor precise factual retrieval while larger chunks
(512–1024 tokens) better support questions requiring broader context. 
11
3.5 Domain-Specific Applications
RAG has been successfully applied across numerous domains in recent years. In the
biomedical domain, Xiong et al. (2024) demonstrated that RAG-based systems
significantly reduce hallucinations compared to vanilla LLMs when answering clinical
questions. In the legal domain, Louis et al. (2024) built a RAG system for statutory
interpretation that achieved near-expert-level accuracy on legal reasoning benchmarks.
In the educational domain, Abdelghani et al. (2023) applied RAG to build personalized
tutoring systems that answer student questions grounded in course materials, directly
analogous to the system proposed in this project.
3.6 Chunking Strategies
A critical but often overlooked component of RAG systems is the document chunking
strategy — how documents are split into retrievable units. Kamradt (2023) proposed
semantic chunking, where splits occur at semantically meaningful boundaries rather
than fixed token counts, resulting in more coherent retrieved passages. Anthropic
(2024) and other practitioners have explored contextual retrieval, which prepends a
brief context summary to each chunk before embedding, significantly improving
retrieval accuracy for ambiguous passages.

PROJECT OBJECTIVE AND SCOPE
4. Project Objective and Scope
Building on the surveyed literature, this section defines the concrete goals and
boundaries of the proposed system. It outlines the primary objective of the project,
describes the intended scope and constraints, and identifies the target user and use cases
that motivate the design decisions made in subsequent sections.
4.1 Objective
The primary objective of this project is to design and implement a web-based Intelligent
Document Q&A system that enables users to upload academic PDF documents and
query them in natural language. The system will leverage RAG architecture to provide
accurate, context-grounded answers and will display the source passages used to
generate each response, promoting transparency and verifiability.
4.2 Scope
The system will support single-document and multi-document querying, handle PDF
files of up to 50 pages, and provide a clean, intuitive web interface accessible without
technical expertise. The scope is intentionally constrained to English-language academic
documents to maintain focus and manageability within the project timeline. Real-time
indexing (i.e., the user uploads a document and can immediately query it) is a core
requirement.
4.3 Use Case
The primary target user is a student or researcher who needs to quickly extract specific
information from a research paper or report without reading the entire document.
Secondary use cases include comparing information across multiple uploaded
documents and generating summaries of specific sections.

SYSTEM ARCHITECTURE AND FLOWCHART
5. System Architecture and Flowchart
The system follows a two-phase RAG pipeline: an offline indexing phase (triggered
when a document is uploaded) and an online querying phase (triggered when the user
submits a question).
5.1 Indexing Phase
• Document Upload — The user uploads a PDF file through the web interface.
• Text Extraction — The PDF is parsed and raw text is extracted using
PyMuPDF or pdfplumber.
• Text Chunking — The extracted text is split into overlapping chunks of
approximately 500 tokens with a 50-token overlap to preserve context at
boundaries.
• Embedding Generation — Each chunk is converted into a dense vector
representation using a sentence embedding model (sentence-transformers/allMiniLM-L6-v2).
• Vector Storage — The chunk embeddings and their corresponding text are
stored in a FAISS vector index, along with metadata (page number, document
name).
5.2 Querying Phase
• User Query — The user types a natural language question in the chat interface.
• Query Embedding — The query is embedded using the same sentence
embedding model.
• Similarity Search — The query vector is compared against all chunk vectors in
the FAISS index using cosine similarity; the top-5 most similar chunks are
retrieved.
• Prompt Construction — A structured prompt is built, inserting the retrieved
chunks as context along with the user's question and a system instruction to
answer only based on the provided context. 
14
• LLM Generation — The prompt is sent to Anthropic's Claude API (claudehaiku-4-5), which generates a grounded natural language answer.
• Response Display — The answer is displayed in the chat interface along with
the retrieved source chunks highlighted as references.

EXPECTED OUTCOMES AND CHALLANGES
7. Expected Outcomes and Challenges
This section discusses what the completed system is expected to achieve and identifies
the key technical challenges that are likely to arise during development. Understanding
these challenges in advance allows for more informed design decisions and sets realistic
expectations for the project's scope and limitations.
7.1 Expected Outcomes
Upon completion, the system is expected to accurately answer factual questions
grounded in uploaded documents with minimal hallucination, provide source references
for every generated answer to support user verification, handle multi-document queries
by retrieving relevant chunks across the entire indexed corpus, and demonstrate
measurable improvement over a no-RAG baseline (direct LLM querying without
retrieval) on a set of manually curated test questions. 
17
7.2 Anticipated Challenges
Several technical challenges are anticipated. First, chunking granularity presents a
trade-off: overly small chunks may lack sufficient context while overly large chunks
may dilute retrieval precision. An ablation study comparing chunk sizes of 256, 512,
and 1024 tokens is planned. Second, query-document vocabulary mismatch may
occur when users phrase questions differently from the language used in the document;
query rewriting or HyDE (Hypothetical Document Embeddings) may be explored as a
mitigation. Third, multi-hop questions — those requiring synthesis of information
from multiple non-adjacent passages — are inherently harder for single-step retrieval;
this represents a known limitation of Naive RAG that may be partially addressed
through larger top-k retrieval. Fourth, PDF layout complexity (tables, figures, multicolumn text) can degrade text extraction quality, which will need to be handled through
preprocessing heuristics.
